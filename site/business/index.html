Three jobs, and the ones I left alone

Lockin works. Nobody knew it existed. This page is what I did about that — who it is for, the three launch jobs I actually ran, what each one produced, and the five I deliberately skipped.

Expectation 1 · say who it's for, clearly enough that some people are left out

One student, at one desk, losing to one phone

Indian undergrads in exam season — JEE, NEET, GATE, CA, semester finals. 17 to 23, studying three or more hours a day at a desk with their phone face-up beside them. They already watch study-with-me videos. They tried Forest and quit when killing a tree stopped meaning anything. They study alone, and they'd rather not.

What that person does today, without Lockin A phone timer they ignore, a lo-fi tab, a WhatsApp group where six people type “studying” and then don't, and no honest record on Sunday of whether those four hours were four hours.

Built for

  • Students who want to be measured, not blocked
  • People who study alone but want someone on the other end of the clock
  • Anyone who won't make an account for a timer
  • Phone-in-hand studiers who need to start without touching anything

Not built for — and I'd rather say it

  • Working professionals. The vocabulary is streaks, XP and levels. It's a student product.
  • Teams and companies. No seats, no admin, no shared workspace.
  • Multi-device users. Data lives in one browser. There is no sync, and there won't be.
  • Group study rooms. Together is strictly one-on-one. Ten people can't join.
  • Anyone who wants an app blocker. Lockin never stops you.

It doesn't block you.
It counts you.

Expectation 2 · choose the jobs your launch needs

Community, marketing, pricing

Three jobs, and they are three different kinds of work. Community is a room that has to be kept alive. Marketing is a loudspeaker that only works while you're holding it. Pricing is a single decision you publish once and never revisit.

They are in this order because that is the order they depend on each other: pricing is the sentence the other two reuse, community is the thing marketing has to deliver people into, and marketing is worthless if the other two aren't ready to catch what it sends.

CommunityThe home

Why I chose it: this isn't a marketing channel, it's a missing product feature shipped as a room. Lockin's differentiating feature — the duel — needs a second person, and the app has no way to find one. The Rivals screen literally says “No rivals yet.” That's not a promotion problem, it's an unfulfilled dependency visible in the interface.

Building matchmaking properly needs a server, presence, a queue and accounts — all four of which destroy the zero-cost architecture and the privacy promise. So this was a build-versus-buy decision where I chose neither: I borrowed infrastructure someone else runs, for ₹0, and got most of the value in an hour.

The three design decisions worth defending

Seven channels, not twenty

Perceived liveliness is messages divided by channels. The same twenty messages look like a community in seven rooms and a ghost town in thirty. There is no #general and no #off-topic — every channel either produces a duel or builds trust.

A channel specifically for failure

#autopsies exists because a server where everyone is winning is unbelievable, and it's a miserable place to sit when you've had a bad week — which pushes out exactly the people the product is for. Participation norms get set by the lowest bar you publicly accept, so the pin states the bar out loud.

The invite is wired into the app in four places

No accounts means no email list, so the invite is the only way to reach a user a second time. It sits in the tab bar, under “No rivals yet” on Together, on the session-complete card, and in the footer. It is also a permanent invite — a default Discord link expires after seven days and takes every copy of itself with it, including the one inside the app.

MarketingThe loudspeaker

Why I chose it: it's the only job that produces first-time visitors at speed, and I have two hooks nothing else in the category has — you start the timer by talking to it, and two people can race the same clock over one link. Both are visible in three seconds, which is all a scroll gives you.

The binding constraint was production capacity, not ideas: one person, no budget, and a Discord ritual to run every evening. So I optimised for deriving assets rather than making them — two animated ads became roughly thirty postable pieces through ratio cuts, length cuts and frame extraction, because every keyframe of an animation is already a designed composition.

PricingThe promise

Why I chose it: it takes an afternoon and its output is a sentence every other job reuses. A launch that can't say what it costs looks unfinished — and “free” is only a real answer if you can say why.

Why Lockin is free

The instinct is to defend free on cost: no server, nothing to recover. That's true and it's the wrong opening. Cost explains why free is possible, not why it's right, and one question goes straight through it — plenty of cheap products still charge. So the cost fact goes last.

Lockin's product isn't the timer. It's the person on the other end of the link. There are thousands of study timers and most are free; the only thing Lockin has that they don't is a second person. That makes it a network good — its value to any one member rises with the number of other members — and it changes what a price is.

A price is a filter, not revenue

In an ordinary product a lost sale costs the seller one sale. In a network good a lost member costs every remaining member something: one fewer person online at 9 PM, one longer wait before anyone takes your link. The arithmetic isn't “₹50 × the people who pay” — it's that minus a worse product for all of them.

A price lands exactly where the loop is weakest

Lockin grows one way: someone sends a link to someone who doesn't use it. That second person has no trust and thirty seconds of patience, and a paywall lands on precisely them. If enough decline, each user recruits fewer than one more and growth doesn't slow — it ends.

A price changes what the room is

Someone who pays is a customer and expects service. Someone who joins free is a participant — they take a stranger's duel, they post the session where they managed 12 minutes out of 45. Every ritual in the server depends on reciprocity, and money reframes every interaction it touches.

And then, last: there is no bill

Static site, browser-to-browser duels, every session stored in the user's own browser. The thousandth user consumes what the first did: nothing. Most free products are free until — until the runway ends, until the bill lands. Lockin has no such moment scheduled, which is why it can be promised permanently rather than hoped for.

Never charged for, written down so it's expensive to break: starting a timer, duelling a friend, voice control, insights, achievements, the journal. And no ads — that one isn't conditional. An advertisement inside a focus app is a contradiction.

Expectation 3 · make things that work while you're not standing next to them

What keeps running when I close the laptop

The test I applied to everything: if I stopped tonight, does it still do its job tomorrow? Five things pass.

The thingWhat it does without me
The Discord invite inside the appEvery person who finishes a session or opens an empty Rivals screen is offered somewhere to find a partner. No post required.
A permanent invite linkNever expires and has unlimited uses, so every copy already in the wild keeps working — the default seven-day link would have died silently.
Pinned messages in all seven channelsA new member learns the duel handshake, the one rule and the 8 PM ritual without anyone being online to explain it.
The Instagram grid bannersThree spanning rows that keep arguing at anyone who lands on the profile from any post, months after the post itself is buried.
The one that took a real decision: mentions-only notifications on the server. Discord's default pings every member for every message, new members mute within two days and never return. One invisible setting decides whether the ritual has an audience at all.

Deliverable 2 · the short note

Who it's for, what I picked, what I dropped

Lockin — launch note
  • Who it's for. Indian undergrads in exam season, 17 to 23, who study three or more hours a day at a desk with their phone face-up beside them. They tried Forest and quit. They study alone and would rather not.
  • Who it's not for. Working professionals, teams, anyone who needs it on two devices, anyone who wants a group room, and anyone who wants an app blocker. Lockin never stops you — it counts you.
  • Jobs picked — three. Community, because the duel needs a second person and the app has no way to find one; the server is a missing feature, not a marketing channel. Marketing, because voice control and the duel are hooks nobody else has and the footage already existed. Pricing, because the answer is a sentence every other job reuses.
  • Jobs dropped — five. Marketplace, because a listing sends strangers to an app with an empty Rivals list. Sales, because the only yes worth closing is the duel handshake and the community produces it faster. Support, absorbed into one channel with a 24-hour fix promise. PR and Partners, both held behind stated triggers because neither has anything to work with yet.
How I'll know it worked: five people posting a seven-day streak. Followers, impressions and member counts are all easier to get and none of them mean the product is doing its job.
Lockin — a free focus timer you run by voice and race a friend on. No account, no install, no backend. Built by a student, launched in public, numbers reported as they are.
